The key distinctionDominance = interaction between alleles of the SAME locus. Epistasis = interaction between genes of DIFFERENT loci, where one gene (epistatic) masks the expression of another (hypostatic). All modified ratios still total 16 — only the grouping changes.
6 PLEIOTROPY · PENETRANCE · EXPRESSIVITY
  • Pleiotropy — one gene produces several unrelated phenotypic effects. Ex: Dexter gene (short legs + bulldog lethal), Merle in dogs (coat + deafness + eye defects), Manx cat (taillessness + lethal), Frizzle fowl (feather + metabolic rate), dwarfism genes.
  • Penetrance — the PERCENTAGE of individuals carrying a genotype that actually show the phenotype. Complete (100%) or incomplete (e.g. 80% penetrance = 8 of 10 carriers show it). A population/all-or-none concept.
  • Expressivity — the DEGREE or intensity to which the phenotype is expressed in an individual that does show it. Variable expressivity: white spotting in Holsteins, polydactyly, roan intensity. An individual/graded concept.
  • Why they vary: modifier genes, genetic background, sex, age, nutrition, temperature and other environmental effects.
RememberPenetrance = "HOW MANY" (yes/no, %). Expressivity = "HOW MUCH" (mild → severe). Both together explain why a known carrier may look completely normal.
7 MULTIPLE ALLELES & BLOOD GROUPS

Multiple alleles = three or more alternative forms of a gene at the same locus on homologous chromosomes, arising by repeated mutation. An individual (diploid) can carry only TWO of them; a population carries all. They show a definite dominance series and always affect the same character.

  • Rabbit coat colour: C (full agouti) > cch (chinchilla) > ch (Himalayan) > c (albino).
  • Human ABO: IA = IB > i — IA&IB codominant, both dominant to i.
  • Cattle blood groups: 11 systems — A, B, C, F, J, L, M, S, Z, R′S′, T′. The B system is the most complex known in any animal (>1000 alleles/phenogroups).
  • Applications: parentage verification & pedigree confirmation in AI programmes; detecting freemartin/chimerism; blood transfusion; genetic markers for breed characterisation; twin diagnosis (monozygotic vs dizygotic).
  • J substance in cattle is a soluble antigen adsorbed onto RBC; haemolytic disease of the newborn in mule foals and piglets arises from blood-group incompatibility.
8 LETHAL GENES

A lethal gene kills the individual carrying it in the appropriate genotype, usually before reproductive age.

TypeEffect
Dominant lethalHomozygote dies; heterozygote shows a marker phenotype → 2 : 1 ratio. Ex: Dexter bulldog calf, yellow mouse, Creeper fowl.
Recessive lethalOnly homozygous recessive dies; carriers normal → hidden in the population. Ex: bovine achondroplasia, amputated, muscular hypertrophy, BLAD, CVM, DUMPS in Holstein.
Sub-lethal / semi-lethalKills some carriers; survival < 100%.
Conditional lethalLethal only in a particular environment.
Breeding relevanceRecessive lethals persist because heterozygous carriers are phenotypically normal. They surface when inbreeding rises or when a popular AI sire is widely used. Detection = test mating / progeny testing or, today, DNA carrier testing; control = screen and cull carrier sires.

The mammalian rule — say thisIn mammals the Y chromosome is male-determining because it carries the SRY gene (Sex-determining Region of Y) = TDF on its short arm. SRY switches the indifferent gonad into a testis → Sertoli cells secrete AMH/MIS (regresses Müllerian ducts) and Leydig cells secrete testosterone (develops Wolffian ducts). No SRY → ovary by default. Hence XXY is male (Klinefelter) and XO is female (Turner) — unlike Drosophila, where the X : A ratio decides.

Intersex conditions in livestock: FREEMARTIN — the sterile female of a heterosexual bovine twin pair; anastomosis of foetal placental vessels lets male cells and hormones cross → XX/XY blood chimerism, hypoplastic tubular tract; ~92% of heifers born twin to a bull. Others: polled intersex in goats (linked to the polled gene), XXY (Klinefelter, sterile bull), 63,XO Turner in mares, true and pseudo-hermaphrodites, testicular feminisation.

10 DOSAGE COMPENSATION & LYON HYPOTHESIS

Problem: females have two X chromosomes and males only one — yet the amount of X-gene product is equal in both sexes. The mechanism that equalises it is dosage compensation.

Lyon hypothesis (Mary Lyon, 1961): in every somatic cell of a female mammal, one X is randomly inactivated early in embryonic life; the inactivation is random (paternal or maternal), permanent, and clonally inherited by all descendant cells. The condensed inactive X = BARR BODY (sex chromatin), seen at the nuclear membrane; also visible as the "drumstick" in neutrophils.

Rule Number of Barr bodies = (Number of X chromosomes) − 1

XX = 1 · XY = 0 · XO (Turner) = 0 · XXY (Klinefelter) = 1 · XXX = 2.

The classic proofTortoiseshell / calico cats are almost always female. The orange (O) and black (o) alleles are X-linked; random X-inactivation in different patches of skin gives a mosaic coat. A male tortoiseshell is rare and sterile — he is XXY. This single example proves both X-linkage and random X-inactivation.

Note: in birds (ZZ/ZW) dosage compensation is incomplete. Barr-body counting was the classical sexing method before molecular tests.

12 LINKAGE & CROSSING OVER

Linkage = the tendency of genes located on the same chromosome to be inherited together, i.e. they do not assort independently. Discovered by Bateson & Punnett (1906, sweet pea); explained by T.H. Morgan (Drosophila) — the chromosome theory of linkage. All genes on one chromosome form a linkage group; number of linkage groups = haploid number (n) — cattle 30, buffalo 25, chicken 39.

TermMeaning
Coupling (cis)Both dominants on one chromosome: AB/ab
Repulsion (trans)One dominant on each: Ab/aB
Complete linkageNo crossing over; only parental types (♂ Drosophila, ♀ silkworm)
Incomplete linkageParental types in excess + a few recombinants — the usual situation

Crossing over = reciprocal exchange of segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes at PACHYTENE of prophase I; the visible point of exchange is the CHIASMA (seen at diplotene). Types: single, double, multiple. Factors affecting it: distance between genes (↑ distance ↑ CO), sex, age, temperature, X-rays and chemicals, proximity to the centromere (reduced).

SignificanceCrossing over is the chief source of genetic recombination — it creates new gene combinations, allows linkage mapping, breaks unfavourable linkages, and underlies marker-assisted selection.
13 LINKAGE MAP — RECOMBINATION FREQUENCY
RF Recombination frequency % = (No. of recombinant progeny / Total progeny) × 100 Map distance 1% RF = 1 map unit (m.u.) = 1 centiMorgan (cM)
  • Maximum RF = 50%. At 50% the genes behave as if unlinked (independent assortment) — either on different chromosomes or very far apart on the same one.
  • Recombination frequency is directly proportional to the distance between genes (Sturtevant, 1913) — this is what makes mapping possible.
  • Linkage maps are constructed from a THREE-POINT TEST CROSS: the two rarest (double-crossover) classes identify the MIDDLE gene; the two most frequent classes are parental.
  • Strength of linkage ∝ 1/distance. Closely linked genes → few recombinants.
Coincidence C.o.C. = Observed double CO / Expected double CO Interference I = 1 − C.o.C.

Interference = one crossover reduces the chance of a second nearby crossover (positive interference is the rule). Expected DCO = product of the two individual RFs.
Map types: genetic/linkage map (distance in cM, from RF) vs physical map (distance in base pairs, from sequencing) — the two do not always agree because CO frequency varies along the chromosome.

17 EXTRA-CHROMOSOMAL (CYTOPLASMIC) INHERITANCE

Inheritance controlled by genes located outside the nucleus — in mitochondria (and plastids in plants) — hence also called cytoplasmic, maternal or non-Mendelian inheritance.

  • CRITERIA (how you recognise it): ① Reciprocal crosses give DIFFERENT results ② the phenotype always follows the FEMALE (maternal) parent ③ no Mendelian segregation ratios in F₂ ④ the trait cannot be mapped to any chromosome ⑤ nuclear substitution/back-crossing does not change it.
  • Why maternal? The ovum contributes virtually all the cytoplasm (and therefore all the mitochondria); the sperm contributes essentially only its nucleus, and the few paternal mitochondria are destroyed.
  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA): circular, double-stranded, ~16.3 kb, naked (no histones), 37 genes (13 proteins of oxidative phosphorylation + 22 tRNA + 2 rRNA), high mutation rate, many copies per cell, heteroplasmy possible.
  • Examples: milk-yield and growth maternal/cytoplasmic effects in cattle · mtDNA haplotypes used for breed origin, phylogeny and biodiversity studies · Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy, mitochondrial myopathies · kappa particles in Paramecium · shell coiling in Limnaea and pigment in Ephestia (maternal-effect genes) · male sterility in plants.
  • Distinguish: maternal EFFECT = nuclear genes of the mother acting through the egg cytoplasm (e.g. dam's milk on calf growth) — it is still Mendelian; cytoplasmic inheritance = genes actually located in the cytoplasm.

18 NUCLEIC ACIDS · GENE CONCEPT · REPLICATION

DNA (Watson & Crick, 1953): double helix, right-handed (B-form), antiparallel (5′→3′ and 3′→5′), sugar-phosphate backbone outside, bases inside. 10 bp per turn, pitch 3.4 nm, rise 0.34 nm, diameter 2 nm. Base pairing: A = T (2 H bonds), G ≡ C (3 H bonds). Chargaff's rules: A = T, G = C, purines = pyrimidines. Building block = nucleotide (base + deoxyribose + phosphate); base + sugar = nucleoside.

Gene concept: the unit of heredity — a DNA segment coding for one polypeptide or functional RNA. Beadle & Tatum: one gene–one enzyme → one gene–one polypeptide. Benzer: cistron (function), recon, muton. Eukaryotic genes are split — exons interrupted by introns removed by splicing. Regulation: lac operon (Jacob & Monod, 1961).

DNA REPLICATION — semiconservative (proved by Meselson & Stahl, 1958, 15N density-gradient in E. coli): each daughter molecule has one parental + one new strand. It is bidirectional, starts at an origin, always proceeds 5′ → 3′.

20 POPULATION GENETICS — GENE FREQUENCY & THE HARDY–WEINBERG LAW ★★ CERTAIN QUESTION

Population genetics studies the genetic composition of a Mendelian population — a group of interbreeding individuals sharing a common GENE POOL — and how it changes. Individual genetics asks "what genotype?"; population genetics asks "at what frequency?" Animal breeding is applied population genetics: we change gene frequencies in a herd, not in one cow.

Genotypic freq. D (AA) + H (Aa) + R (aa) = 1 Gene (allele) freq. p = D + ½H  ;  q = R + ½H  ;  p + q = 1 By gene counting p = (2×AA + Aa) / 2N HARDY–WEINBERG p² (AA) + 2pq (Aa) + q² (aa) = 1

Statement (Hardy & Weinberg, 1908, independently): "In a large, random-mating population free from mutation, migration, selection and drift, the gene and genotypic frequencies remain CONSTANT generation after generation." Such a population is in genetic equilibrium.

SIX CONDITIONS: ① Large population (infinitely large) ② Random mating (panmixis) ③ No mutation ④ No migration (no gene flow) ⑤ No selection — all genotypes equally fertile & viable ⑥ No genetic drift; plus normal meiosis, diploid, sexual, non-overlapping generations.

  • Equilibrium is reached in ONE generation of random mating for an autosomal locus, whatever the starting genotypic frequencies. For a sex-linked locus it is approached gradually, the frequency oscillating and halving the difference each generation.
  • Maximum heterozygosity 2pq = 0.5, occurring when p = q = 0.5.
  • When q is small, most recessive alleles are hidden in heterozygotes: at q = 0.01, carriers (2pq ≈ 0.0198) are ≈ 198 times as frequent as affected homozygotes (q² = 0.0001). This is why culling only affected animals removes a recessive defect so slowly.
  • Testing for equilibrium: compute p and q from the data, get expected numbers (Np², N2pq, Nq²), apply χ² = Σ(O−E)²/E with df = no. of genotypes − no. of alleles = 3 − 2 = 1 (compare 3.84).
  • Applications: estimating carrier frequency of recessive lethals and of blood-group/coat-colour alleles; predicting genotype frequencies in a herd; measuring genetic variability; a baseline against which the effect of selection, drift or inbreeding is measured; forensic and parentage probabilities.
TrapInbreeding changes GENOTYPIC frequencies but NOT gene frequencies — it only redistributes: AA = p² + pqF, Aa = 2pq(1 − F), aa = q² + pqF. Only selection, mutation, migration and drift change gene frequency. Also: a population showing HWE proportions is not proof of no evolution — the test has low power.

GENETIC DRIFT
(Sewall Wright effect)		σ²q = pq / 2NDISPERSIVE — magnitude predictable but direction random. Random change in gene frequency due to sampling of gametes in a SMALL population. Leads to random fixation (q=1) or loss (q=0), ↑ homozygosity, ↓ variability, differentiation between herds. Includes the FOUNDER EFFECT (new herd started from few animals) and BOTTLENECK (population crash). Critical for small closed herds, zoos and endangered breeds.
The one-line classification they wantSYSTEMATIC forces — mutation, migration, selection: both the amount and the direction of change are predictable. DISPERSIVE force — random genetic drift: only the amount (variance) is predictable, the direction is random. Together with inbreeding (which alters genotypic, not gene, frequency) these are the complete set of forces acting on a gene pool.
Put the four together in a herdA closed Sahiwal herd of 40 cows: drift randomly loses rare alleles; inbreeding rises, exposing recessive defects; selection for milk yield shifts the favourable alleles up; introducing outside bulls = migration, which restores variability and can rapidly change frequencies; mutation quietly supplies the raw material. Effective population size (Ne) is the number that decides how strong drift and inbreeding will be — the link into Unit 3 conservation.

TrapAlmost every trait of economic importance is QUANTITATIVE — which is exactly why animal breeding needs statistics (Unit 1) rather than Punnett squares. Threshold traits (twinning, dystocia, disease resistance) look qualitative but have an underlying continuous liability.

Genotypic values (Falconer): midpoint m between the two homozygotes; AA = +a, aa = −a, Aa = d. d = 0 no dominance · d = a complete dominance · d > a overdominance.

Population mean M = a(p − q) + 2pqd Average effect of gene substitution α = a + d(q − p) Breeding value BV = sum of the average effects of the genes an animal carries
AA = 2qα · Aa = (q − p)α · aa = −2pα

Why BV matters: a parent passes on genes, not genotypes — dominance and epistatic combinations are broken up at meiosis. So only the additive part is transmitted. BV = twice the mean deviation of an animal's progeny from the herd mean; the estimated BV is what we now call the EBV, and it is the value on which all modern selection (BLUP, genomic selection) is based. Transmitting ability = ½ BV.

23 COMPONENTS OF VARIANCE & G × E ★ DRAW THIS TREE
P = G + E + G×E  →  VP = VG + VE + VGE
VG = VA + VD + VI
VE = VEp + VEt
VA additive — fixable, transmitted, the ONLY part selection uses
VEp permanent environment (non-fixable but persists on the same animal)
VD dominance (interaction within a locus) — exploited by crossbreeding/heterosis
VEt temporary environment (season, lactation, feeding of the day)
VI epistatic (interaction between loci) — also non-fixable
VGE genotype × environment interaction

G × E INTERACTION = the ranking or relative performance of genotypes changes from one environment to another; i.e. the difference between two genotypes is not the same in all environments. Detected when reaction norms cross, or when the genetic correlation of the same trait measured in two environments is well below 1 (rA < 0.8 is taken as important G×E).

The classic Indian example — use itHolstein-Friesian cattle rank first for milk yield in a temperate, well-managed environment but may rank below Sahiwal or crossbreds under tropical heat, poor feeding and high disease challenge. Hence: select animals in the environment in which their progeny will actually be kept; imported semen from temperate progeny tests may not perform here. Also seen in poultry strains under cage vs deep-litter, and in G×E for sheep in hill vs plain systems. Reducing G×E: separate breeding programmes per production system, or select for robustness/adaptability.

Genotype–environment CORRELATION (different from interaction) = better genotypes systematically receive better environments (e.g. the best cows are given the best feed) — it inflates apparent genetic differences and must be removed by contemporary comparison / random allotment.

24 HERITABILITY (h²) ★★ + REPEATABILITY (r)
Narrow sense h² h² = VA / VP  — the one always meant in animal breeding Broad sense H² H² = VG / VP = (VA+VD+VI) / VP Repeatability r = (VG + VEp) / VP  →  r ≥ h² always

h² = the proportion of the total phenotypic variance that is due to additive genetic (transmissible) effects; equivalently the regression of breeding value on phenotype. It is a property of a trait IN A POPULATION IN AN ENVIRONMENT, not of an individual; ranges 0 to 1; it is not a constant.

25 REPEATABILITY · CORRELATIONS · LAST-10-MINUTES SWEEP

REPEATABILITY (r) = the correlation between REPEATED RECORDS of the SAME trait on the SAME individual (successive lactations, successive clips, successive litters). It measures the fraction of variance due to permanent differences (genetic + permanent environmental). Estimated as the intraclass correlation from an ANOVA of repeated records. Uses: ① sets the upper limit of h² ② decides how many records are worth taking ③ allows early culling on first-lactation records ④ used to compute MPPA. Values: milk yield ≈ 0.4–0.5, fat % ≈ 0.6, litter size ≈ 0.15.

MPPA MPPA = P̄ + [ nr / (1 + (n−1)r) ] × (P̄i − P̄)

GENETIC CORRELATION (rA) = correlation between the breeding values of two different traits; caused by PLEIOTROPY (mainly) and by linkage (temporary). PHENOTYPIC (rP) and ENVIRONMENTAL (rE) correlations complete the set.

Relation rP = rA·h₁·h₂ + rE·e₁·e₂ Correlated response CRY = i · hX · hY · rA · σPY

Uses of rA: predicts the correlated response in a second trait · allows indirect selection when the target trait is hard/late/sex-limited to measure · essential for building a selection index · warns of antagonistic pairs. Classic negative (antagonistic) rA: milk yield vs fat %, milk yield vs fertility, growth rate vs feed efficiency in poultry, egg number vs egg size. Positive: body weight and growth rate; milk yield and SNF yield.
