1 WHY BIOSTATISTICS · SCOPE & LIMITATIONS

Statistics = science of collection, organisation, presentation, analysis and interpretation of numerical data. Biostatistics = statistics applied to biological, medical and veterinary data (Bio + Statistics; coined from the variability of living material).

  • Why needed: biological data are inherently variable — no two animals give identical yield; conclusions must be drawn from samples, not whole populations.
  • Uses in vet science: comparing rations/treatments · estimating heritability & breeding values · designing field trials · disease prevalence & morbidity–mortality rates · standardising lactation yield · drug efficacy trials · sire evaluation.
  • Limitations: deals with aggregates, not individuals; only quantitative facts; results are true only on average; can be misused; requires expert handling.
Definition to quoteCroxton & Cowden: "Statistics is the science of collection, presentation, analysis and interpretation of numerical data."

5 MEASURES OF CENTRAL TENDENCY — FORMULA VAULT

A single value around which the data cluster. Requisites of an ideal average (Yule): rigidly defined · based on all observations · easy to understand & compute · suitable for further algebraic treatment · least affected by sampling fluctuation · not unduly affected by extremes.

MEAN simple x̄ = Σx / n MEAN grouped x̄ = Σfx / Σf  (x = class mid-point) Step-deviation x̄ = A + (Σfd / Σf) × c , d = (x − A)/c MEDIAN simple odd: [(n+1)/2]ᵗʰ item · even: mean of (n/2) & (n/2 + 1)ᵗʰ MEDIAN grouped M = L + [ (N/2 − cf) / f ] × h MODE grouped Z = L + [ (f₁ − f₀) / (2f₁ − f₀ − f₂) ] × h Empirical relation Mode = 3 Median − 2 Mean Quartiles Q₁ = L + [(N/4 − cf)/f]h ; Q₃ uses 3N/4

Other averages: GM = ⁿ√(x₁x₂…xₙ) = antilog(Σlog x / n) — used for rates of growth, ratios. HM = n / Σ(1/x) — used for average speed / rates per unit. Relation: AM ≥ GM ≥ HM; for two values GM² = AM × HM.

7 MEASURES OF DISPERSION — FORMULA VAULT

Dispersion / variation = extent to which observations scatter about the average. Two herds may have the same mean but very different uniformity — dispersion measures that.

Coefficient of Variation — full answerCV = (SD/Mean) × 100. It is a relative, unit-free (percentage) measure, so it is the only valid way to compare variability of two series with different units or widely different means (e.g. milk yield in kg vs fat % ; Sahiwal vs HF herd). Lower CV = more consistent / uniform / stable. Used in animal breeding to judge uniformity of a herd, repeatability of a lab technique, and in field trials CV of the experiment indicates experimental precision (a low CV in ANOVA = well-conducted trial).
TrapCV is meaningless if the mean is near zero or negative. Range is the simplest but most unstable (uses only 2 values). SD is the best and most widely used measure of dispersion.
9 MOMENTS · SKEWNESS · KURTOSIS — SHAPE OF THE DISTRIBUTION

Moments describe the shape of a frequency distribution — arithmetic means of powers of deviations.

Central moments μr = Σf(x − x̄)r / N

μ₁ = 0 always · μ₂ = variance (σ²) · μ₃ → skewness · μ₄ → kurtosis. Raw moments μ′r are taken about an arbitrary point A; convert with μ₂ = μ′₂ − μ′₁², μ₃ = μ′₃ − 3μ′₂μ′₁ + 2μ′₁³.

β₁ (skewness) β₁ = μ₃² / μ₂³ ; γ₁ = √β₁ = μ₃/σ³ β₂ (kurtosis) β₂ = μ₄ / μ₂² ; γ₂ = β₂ − 3

SKEWNESS = lack of symmetry (which tail is longer).

10 PROBABILITY — DEFINITIONS & THEOREMS
Classical P(A) = favourable outcomes / total equally-likely outcomes = m/n

0 ≤ P(A) ≤ 1 · P(certain) = 1 · P(impossible) = 0 · P(A) + P(Ā) = 1 (complement).
Empirical / relative-frequency definition: P = lim (m/n) as n → ∞ (von Mises).
Terms: random experiment · trial · sample space (S) · event · mutually exclusive (cannot occur together, A∩B = φ) · exhaustive (cover S) · equally likely · independent (one does not affect the other).

ADDITION (OR) P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) — mutually exclusive P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) MULTIPLICATION (AND) P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B|A) — independent P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B) Conditional P(B|A) = P(A ∩ B)/P(A) Bayes' theorem P(Ai|B) = P(Ai)P(B|Ai) / Σ P(Aj)P(B|Aj)
Genetics application (write this)Monohybrid Aa × Aa → P(aa) = ¼ (classical). Both parents' gametes are independent → multiplication rule. Probability that a calf is either red or polled → addition rule. Mendel's laws are essentially statements of independent probability, which is why probability underpins all of animal genetics.
11 BINOMIAL DISTRIBUTION (Bernoulli, 1713)
pmf P(x) = nCx px qn−x , x = 0,1,2,…,n ; q = 1 − p Mean μ = np    Variance σ² = npq    SD = √npq
  • Conditions: fixed number of trials n · each trial has only two mutually exclusive outcomes (success/failure) · p constant for every trial · trials are independent.
  • Discrete distribution; parameters n and p.
  • Mean > Variance always (because q < 1). This is the identifying line.
  • Symmetrical when p = q = 0.5; positively skewed when p < 0.5.
  • Skewness γ₁ = (q − p)/√npq; Kurtosis β₂ = 3 + (1 − 6pq)/npq.
  • Vet examples: sex ratio of calves born · number of animals responding to a vaccine out of n · Mendelian segregation ratios · number of carriers in a litter.
12 POISSON DISTRIBUTION (1837) — LAW OF RARE EVENTS
pmf P(x) = e−λ λx / x! , x = 0,1,2,… ∞ Key identity Mean = Variance = λ = np  (SD = √λ)
  • Limiting case of binomial when n → very large, p → very small, np = λ finite.
  • Single parameter λ; discrete; range 0 → ∞ (no upper limit, unlike binomial).
  • Always positively skewed; becomes symmetrical as λ increases; γ₁ = 1/√λ, β₂ = 3 + 1/λ.
  • Sum of independent Poisson variates is Poisson (additive property).
  • Vet examples: number of rare congenital defects per 1000 births · deaths from a rare disease · bacterial colonies per plate · somatic cell counts · number of ticks per animal · accidents in a farm per month.
One-mark discriminatorBinomial: mean > variance, finite range 0–n, two parameters. Poisson: mean = variance, infinite range, one parameter. If a question says "rare event / very large n / small p" → Poisson.
13 NORMAL DISTRIBUTION (De Moivre · Laplace · Gauss)
pdf f(x) = 1/(σ√2π) · e−(x−μ)²/2σ² , −∞ < x < ∞

PROPERTIES — write all of these for full marks:

  • Continuous; parameters μ (location) and σ (scale); written X ~ N(μ, σ²).
  • Bell-shaped, perfectly symmetrical about x = μ; unimodal.
  • Mean = Median = Mode = μ; skewness β₁ = 0, kurtosis β₂ = 3 (mesokurtic).
  • Total area under the curve = 1 (or 100%); curve never touches the X-axis — asymptotic to it.
  • Quartiles equidistant: Q₃ − M = M − Q₁; QD = 0.6745σ, MD = 0.7979σ.
  • Points of inflexion at μ ± σ. Odd central moments = 0; μ₄ = 3σ⁴.
  • Linear combination of normal variates is normal. Central Limit Theorem: means of samples from ANY population tend to normal as n increases.
AREA RULE — memorise exactlyμ ± 1σ = 68.27% · μ ± 2σ = 95.45% · μ ± 3σ = 99.73%
Reverse (used in testing): μ ± 1.96σ = 95% · μ ± 2.58σ = 99% · μ ± 1.645σ = 90%.
14 STANDARD NORMAL VARIATE & CURVE
SNV Z = (x − μ) / σ → Z ~ N(0, 1)
  • Standardisation converts any normal curve to one standard curve with mean 0 and SD 1, so a single table serves all problems.
  • Z is a pure number (unit-free) — it tells how many SDs an observation lies from the mean.
  • Area is symmetrical: P(Z > 1.96) = 0.025; P(−1.96 < Z < 1.96) = 0.95.
  • Uses: converting raw scores · finding % of animals above/below a cut-off (basis of selection intensity in breeding!) · normal approximation to binomial (when np > 5 and nq > 5) and to Poisson (λ > 10) · basis of all large-sample tests.
Link to Unit 3The selection differential and intensity of selection (i) come straight from the normal curve: if the top p% are selected, i = z/p, where z is the height of the ordinate at the truncation point. So Unit 1's normal curve is Unit 3's selection theory.

16 CORRELATION

Correlation = degree and direction of linear association between two variables. Studied by scatter diagram (visual), Karl Pearson's r (quantitative), and Spearman's rank rs.

Karl Pearson r = Σ(x−x̄)(y−ȳ) / √[ Σ(x−x̄)² · Σ(y−ȳ)² ] Working form r = Cov(x,y)/(σx·σy) = [nΣxy − ΣxΣy] / √{[nΣx²−(Σx)²][nΣy²−(Σy)²]} Spearman rank rs = 1 − 6Σd² / n(n² − 1) , d = difference of ranks SE / test t = r√(n−2) / √(1−r²) , df = n − 2
  • Limits: −1 ≤ r ≤ +1. +1 = perfect positive, −1 = perfect negative, 0 = no linear relation.
  • r is a pure number, independent of units and of change of origin and scale.
  • r² = coefficient of determination = proportion of variation in Y explained by X (r = 0.8 → 64% explained).
  • Types: positive/negative · linear/non-linear · simple, partial, multiple.
  • Spearman is used for ranked/ordinal or non-normal data (e.g. judging scores of animals in a show) — it is the non-parametric counterpart.
TrapCorrelation ≠ causation. A high r may be due to a spurious/nonsense correlation or a common third cause. Also r = 0 only rules out a linear relation, not a curvilinear one.
17 REGRESSION

Regression (Sir Francis Galton) = the average functional relationship used to predict the dependent variable from the independent one. Lines are fitted by the method of least squares (minimising Σ of squared deviations).

Y on X (Y − Ȳ) = byx(X − X̄) ; byx = Σxy/Σx² = r·(σy/σx) X on Y (X − X̄) = bxy(Y − Ȳ) ; bxy = Σxy/Σy² = r·(σx/σy) Bridge to r r = ± √(byx · bxy) → r is the GM of the two b's
  • Both regression coefficients carry the same sign as r; both cannot exceed 1 numerically at the same time; their product ≤ 1.
  • The two lines always intersect at (X̄, Ȳ).
  • r = ±1 → the two lines coincide; r = 0 → they are perpendicular (parallel to the axes).
  • b is independent of change of origin but NOT of scale (opposite of r, which is independent of both).
  • b has units (kg per day, etc.); r is unit-free.

18 SAMPLING METHODS — INTRODUCTION

Population (universe) = entire group of interest; sample = representative subset; sampling frame = complete list of units; sampling unit; sampling fraction = n/N.

Why sample? saves time, cost, labour · the only option when the test is destructive (slaughter, necropsy) or the population is infinite · allows greater depth and accuracy per unit · gives an estimate of sampling error.

Sampling error = difference between sample estimate and population value; decreases as n increases and is measurable. Non-sampling error = faulty frame, measurement, recording, non-response; increases with n and is NOT measurable. Essentials of good sampling: representativeness, adequacy, independence, homogeneity.

TrapComplete enumeration = census (no sampling error, but costly). Only probability (random) sampling permits valid statistical inference and computation of standard error.
MethodHow it worksWhen used / note
Simple random (SRS)Every unit has an equal and independent chance; lottery method or random-number tables; with or without replacementPopulation homogeneous; needs a complete frame
Stratified randomPopulation divided into homogeneous strata (breed, age, sex), then SRS within each stratum; proportional or optimum allocationPopulation heterogeneous; gives highest precision; guarantees representation of every subgroup

21 LARGE SAMPLE — Z TEST (n > 30)
Single mean Z = (x̄ − μ) / (σ/√n) Two means Z = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂) Single proportion Z = (p − P) / √(PQ/n) Two proportions Z = (p₁ − p₂) / √[ p̂q̂(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) ]

Assumptions: large sample, population normal (or CLT applies), σ known, random independent sampling.
Decision: |Z| > 1.96 → significant at 5%; |Z| > 2.58 → significant at 1%. 95% confidence limits: x̄ ± 1.96 SE.

22 SMALL SAMPLE — STUDENT'S t TEST (W.S. Gosset, 1908)
① One-sample t = (x̄ − μ)/(s/√n) , df = n − 1 ② Two independent t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂)/[Sp√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)] , df = n₁+n₂−2 — pooled variance Sp² = [Σ(x₁−x̄₁)² + Σ(x₂−x̄₂)²] / (n₁ + n₂ − 2) ③ Paired t t = d̄ / (sd/√n) , df = n − 1 (n = pairs)
  • Assumptions: ① sample drawn from a normal population ② observations independent & random ③ σ unknown, estimated by s ④ for two-sample: homogeneity of variances (test first with F-test).
  • t-curve: symmetrical, bell-shaped but flatter with thicker tails than normal; depends only on df; as df → ∞, t → Z (normal).
  • Paired t is used when the same animal is measured twice (before/after treatment) or animals are matched in pairs — it removes between-animal variation and is therefore more powerful.
  • Vet uses: comparing two rations, two drugs, pre- vs post-treatment PCV, comparing a herd mean with a breed standard.
TrapUnpaired data with n₁ ≠ n₂ → two-sample t. Same animals before/after → paired t. Using unpaired t on paired data wastes information and loses precision.
