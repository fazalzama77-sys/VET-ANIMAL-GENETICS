4 SELECTION — TYPES & RESPONSE ★★

SELECTION = allowing certain animals to be the parents of the next generation more often than others — i.e. differential reproduction. It does not create new genes; it only changes gene frequencies by increasing the frequency of favourable alleles. Selection + culling are two sides of the same act.

TypeEffect on the population
Natural vs ArtificialNature favours fitness; man favours economic merit — the two often oppose each other.
DirectionalFavours one extreme → the mean shifts. The normal breeding situation (higher milk, faster growth).
Stabilising (normalising)Favours the intermediate, culls both extremes → variance falls, mean unchanged. Ex: egg weight, birth weight (dystocia at both ends).
Disruptive (diversifying)Favours both extremes → population splits into two groups; used to create divergent lines.
Selection differential S = (mean of selected parents) − (mean of the population) Intensity of selection i = S / σP  (a standardised, unit-free S) RESPONSE per generation R = ΔG = h² × S = i · h² · σP = i · rIA · σA RESPONSE per YEAR ΔG/yr = (i · h² · σP) / L   (L = generation interval) Realised heritability h² = R / S

Generation interval L = average age of the parents when their replacement offspring are born. Cattle 4.5–6 yr · Buffalo 6–8 · Sheep & goat 3–4 · Pig 2 · Poultry 1–1.5 yr. This is why poultry improve fastest and buffalo slowest.

5 FACTORS AFFECTING RESPONSE TO SELECTION ★ WRITE ALL EIGHT
  1. Heritability of the trait — the higher h², the more of the observed superiority is transmitted. Low-h² traits respond poorly to individual selection.
  2. Selection differential / intensity (i) — set by the proportion selected, hence by the species' reproductive rate and culling rate. Poultry and pigs allow high intensity; cattle very little on the female side.
  3. Phenotypic variability (σP) — no variation, no selection. A uniform herd offers little scope.
  4. Generation interval (L) — yearly response is inversely proportional to L; shortening L (young sires, MOET, genomic selection) is often the fastest route to gain.
  5. Accuracy (rIA) — how well the estimated breeding value reflects the true one; raised by more records, more progeny, BLUP and genomic data.
  6. Number of traits selected for — gain per trait falls roughly as 1/√n. Selecting for 4 traits halves the progress in each.
  7. Genetic correlations — a favourable rA gives free correlated gain; an antagonistic one (milk yield vs fertility, growth vs feed efficiency) slows or reverses progress.
  8. Inbreeding, G × E and environment — inbreeding depresses performance; a poor environment masks genetic differences and lowers h²; a selection plateau is reached when additive variance is exhausted.
Exam trapResponse is per generation; gain is quoted per YEAR — always divide by L. The four "levers" in ΔG/yr = i·r·σA/L are the only ways a plan can work: raise i, raise accuracy r, exploit σA, or cut L. Genomic selection wins by cutting L and raising r in young animals.

7 METHODS OF SELECTION FOR SEVERAL TRAITS — SINGLE vs MULTI-TRAIT ★ SURE DIFFERENTIATE
MethodHow it worksMeritDemerit
TANDEMSelect for ONE trait at a time until it reaches the desired level, then move to the nextSimple; full intensity on one trait; fastest gain in that one traitSLOWEST overall; earlier trait slips back; ignores genetic correlations; wastes generations

SELECTION INDEX
(Hazel & Lush, 1943)		All traits combined into a SINGLE SCORE weighted by their heritabilities, genetic & phenotypic correlations and relative economic values; animals ranked on the indexTHEORETICALLY THE MOST EFFICIENT — allows compensation between traits, uses all information, maximises total economic gainNeeds reliable estimates of h², rA, rP and economic weights; all traits must be recorded on every animal before culling; computationally heavy
Index I = b₁P₁ + b₂P₂ + b₃P₃ + … + bnPn

P = phenotypic record of each trait; b = partial regression weight derived from Pb = Gv, where v = relative economic values. Aggregate genotype H = a₁A₁ + a₂A₂ + …; the index is built to maximise the correlation rIH between I and H.

Efficiency order — one line for the examSELECTION INDEX > INDEPENDENT CULLING LEVEL > TANDEM. In practice a combination is used: cull grossly on ICL for cheap early traits (survival, conformation, gross defects), then rank the survivors on a selection index.
Indian dairy exampleA bull-mother index for Sahiwal might weight first-lactation 305-day yield, fat %, age at first calving and calving interval. The National Dairy Plan progeny-testing projects now rank bulls by BLUP-based total merit indices — selection indices computed with an animal model.
TrapThe selection index and BLUP EBV are not opposites — BLUP produces the breeding values that a modern index combines. Also remember ΔG per trait falls as 1/√n, so an index with too many traits improves none of them appreciably: keep the objective to a few economically important traits.

9 INBREEDING — COEFFICIENT F & RELATIONSHIP R ★★

Inbreeding = mating of individuals more closely related to each other than the average of the population from which they come.

Wright's F FX = Σ (½)(n₁ + n₂ + 1) (1 + FA)

n₁, n₂ = number of generations from sire and from dam back to the common ancestor A; FA = inbreeding of that ancestor; Σ = sum over every path through every common ancestor.
Definition of F: the probability that the two alleles at a locus in an individual are IDENTICAL BY DESCENT (i.e. replicas of one allele in a common ancestor). Range 0 → 1.

Coefficient of relationship RXY = 2FXY / √[(1+FX)(1+FY)]

Practical rule: keep ΔF below 1% per generation (≤0.5% is safer).

10 CONSEQUENCES OF INBREEDING & ITS APPLICATIONS

GENETIC CONSEQUENCES:

  • ↑ HOMOZYGOSITY and ↓ heterozygosity — heterozygotes fall by the factor (1 − F): AA = p² + pqF · Aa = 2pq(1 − F) · aa = q² + pqF.
  • Gene frequency is NOT changed — only the genotypic frequencies are redistributed.
  • Exposes deleterious recessives in the homozygous state → hidden genetic defects appear.
  • Splits the population into inbred lines: variance WITHIN lines falls, BETWEEN lines rises.
  • Increases uniformity and PREPOTENCY (a sire's ability to stamp his characters on his progeny).

PHENOTYPIC CONSEQUENCE — INBREEDING DEPRESSION: the decline in mean performance for traits associated with fitness. It is roughly LINEAR with F and is greatest in traits with low h² and high dominance variance: fertility, conception rate, litter size, viability & survivability, growth rate, milk yield. Traits with mainly additive variance (fat %, skeletal measurements, fibre diameter) are hardly affected. Cause: expression of homozygous deleterious recessives (dominance theory) and loss of heterozygote advantage (overdominance) — it is simply the reverse of heterosis.

APPLICATIONS: ① develop INBRED LINES for later crossing to exploit heterosis (poultry, swine, lab animals) ② fix and make uniform a desirable type — how every breed was originally formed ③ detect carriers of undesirable recessives ④ raise prepotency of elite sires ⑤ produce isogenic lab strains ⑥ line breeding to conserve an outstanding ancestor's genes.

TrapLine breeding is a MILD form of inbreeding, not a separate system. Inbreeding itself never improves a herd — it only exposes what is already there, and is safe only with rigorous simultaneous selection and culling.
11 OUTBREEDING · ITS FORMS & CONSEQUENCES
FormDefinition & use
OutcrossingMating of unrelated animals WITHIN the same breed (no common ancestor for 4–6 generations). Restores vigour in an inbred purebred herd without losing breed identity.
CrossbreedingMating animals of two different breeds of the same species. Produces heterosis and combines complementary breed strengths — the mainstay of Indian dairy, pig and poultry production.
Species / genus hybridisationCrossing different species: horse ♀ × ass ♂ = MULE (2n = 63, sterile, great draught vigour); ass ♀ × horse ♂ = hinny; yak × cattle = dzo. Hybrids are usually sterile — chromosome numbers differ.
GRADING UPRepeated use of purebred sires of one breed on a nondescript/native female population, generation after generation. Exotic inheritance: F₁ 50% → 75% → 87.5% → 93.75% → 96.87%. In 4–6 generations the graded animals are practically purebred. Cheapest and quickest way to upgrade a large nondescript population (e.g. grading up nondescript buffaloes with Murrah bulls).

CONSEQUENCES: ↑ heterozygosity and ↓ homozygosity · masks deleterious recessives · improves fertility, viability and vigour (heterosis) · combines the merits of two breeds (complementarity) · gene frequency unchanged in the population as a whole. Drawbacks: loss of uniformity and of breed purity; heterosis is NOT fixable — it halves in F₂ (recombination loss); crossbreds may be poorly adapted; needs continuous supply of purebred parent stock; indiscriminate crossbreeding erodes indigenous germplasm.

12 HETEROSIS (HYBRID VIGOUR) — Shull, 1914 ★★

Definition: the superiority of the crossbred offspring over the AVERAGE of its two parental breeds (mid-parent value) for a given trait. Its opposite is inbreeding depression.

Heterosis % H% = [ (F₁ mean − Mid-parent mean) / Mid-parent mean ] × 100 Genetic basis H ∝ Σ d y²   (d = dominance deviation, y = difference in gene frequency between the breeds)
  • THEORIES: ① Dominance — each parent's dominant favourable alleles mask the other's deleterious recessives (most accepted). ② Overdominance — the heterozygote Aa is itself superior. ③ Epistasis — favourable between-locus interactions.
  • Two requirements: the trait must show DOMINANCE (d ≠ 0) and the two breeds must DIFFER IN GENE FREQUENCY (y ≠ 0). Hence heterosis is largest between genetically distant breeds.
  • Greatest in LOW-h² fitness traits — fertility, litter size, viability, longevity (10–30%); small in high-h² traits — fat %, fibre diameter, body measurements.
  • Types: individual (in the crossbred itself), maternal (from a crossbred dam — mothering ability, milk), paternal (from a crossbred sire — libido, semen).
  • NOT FIXABLE: in F₂ heterosis halves with heterozygosity (recombination loss) — crossbreds must be regenerated each generation or stabilised into a synthetic.
13 SYSTEMS OF UTILISING HETEROSIS + COMBINING ABILITY (RS & RRS)
SystemHow it works / where used
Two-breed (simple) crossA × B → F₁ all marketed. Maximum individual heterosis, no maternal heterosis.
Three-breed cross(A × B) ♀ × C ♂ — gains individual + maternal heterosis. Common in pigs.
Criss-cross / rotational (2-breed)Crossbred females bred alternately to A and B sires; retains ≈ 67% heterosis, replacements bred on the farm.
Rotational 3-breedSires of A, B, C used in rotation; retains ≈ 86% heterosis.
Terminal (static terminal) crossingAll crossbred progeny go for slaughter; females bought in. Maximum heterosis + complementarity; no replacements produced.
Roto-terminalRotation to breed replacements + a terminal sire on the older females. Best of both.
SYNTHETIC / COMPOSITE breedCrossbreds inter-se mated and stabilised into a new breed — retains part of the heterosis permanently. Karan Swiss, Karan Fries, Frieswal, Sunandini, Vrindavani, Taylor, Jersind; Avikalin, Bharat Merino, Hissardale; Vanaraja, Gramapriya.
Topcross · Incross · IncrossbredInbred sire × non-inbred dams; crosses of inbred lines of the same or different breeds — poultry.

COMBINING ABILITY: GCA = the average performance of a line in all its crosses → due to ADDITIVE gene action. SCA = the deviation of one particular cross from what its parents' GCA predicted → due to DOMINANCE & EPISTASIS.

RS (Recurrent Selection) — one line is selected on its cross performance with a constant inbred tester; improves GCA of that line only. RRS (Reciprocal Recurrent Selection — Comstock, Hess & Bell, 1949) — two lines are each used as the tester for the other, and both are selected and improved simultaneously; improves BOTH GCA and SCA. RRS is the standard method in commercial poultry (and pig) line development.

16 SIRE EVALUATION ★★ — WHY, AND THE METHODS

Why evaluate sires? A bull produces no milk himself (milk is a sex-limited trait), yet through AI one bull can sire thousands of daughters. Over 75–90% of the genetic improvement in a dairy population comes through the sire path. He must therefore be evaluated on his daughters.

MethodBasis & limitation
Dam–daughter comparisonDaughters' average vs their dams' average. Simple but ignores environmental & year differences and regression to the mean.
Simple daughter averageOnly the daughters' mean — badly biased by herd and management.
Equal-parent indexSI = 2D̄ − Dam average (assumes both parents contribute equally).
CONTEMPORARY COMPARISON
(Robertson & Rendel, 1954)		Each daughter is compared with the contemporaries — unrelated herdmates of the same herd, year and season calving at the same time. Removes herd-year-season environmental effects; the standard field method. Weighted by number of daughters.
Herdmate comparison (USDA)A refinement of CC using a wider herdmate base.
Least-squares (Harvey)Handles unequal and disproportionate subclass numbers; fits fixed effects simultaneously.
★ BLUP
(Henderson, 1949/1975)		Best Linear Unbiased Prediction. A mixed-model method that simultaneously estimates the fixed environmental effects and predicts the breeding values. It accounts for all genetic relationships among animals, unequal numbers, non-random mating, culling and genetic trend. Best = minimum error variance · Linear · Unbiased · Prediction of random effects. The ANIMAL MODEL form ranks every animal — cows and bulls — on one scale. Now universally used; combined with SNP data as genomic BLUP / ssGBLUP.
Accuracy r = √[ n / (n + k) ] , k = (4 − h²)/h²  → more daughters, more accuracy

Requirements for a valid progeny test: random allotment of semen across herds, at least 30–50 daughters in 10+ herds, accurate pedigree & milk recording, contemporaries available, and correction for age, season, lactation number and lactation length (305-day, 2× milking, mature-equivalent).

17 OPEN NUCLEUS BREEDING SYSTEM (ONBS) + NEW BREED DEVELOPMENT

ONBS = a breeding structure in which the genetically best animals of the whole population are gathered into a small NUCLEUS herd/flock where intensive recording and selection are done; superior genes then flow down to the base. The nucleus is OPEN because superior females (and males) from the base population are continuously allowed to migrate INTO the nucleus (typically about half of nucleus replacements).

  • Advantages: much higher selection intensity (choose from the whole population, not one herd) · shorter generation interval · greater accuracy (recording is feasible in a small nucleus) · faster genetic gain, especially where field recording is poor — ideal for Indian village conditions · less inbreeding than a CLOSED nucleus because of continuous inflow · rapid dissemination through AI and MOET.
  • Closed nucleus = no inflow → higher intensity but rising inbreeding and loss of variability. MOET-ONBS multiplies the female contribution and is used in Indian buffalo, sheep and goat improvement.

DEVELOPMENT OF NEW BREEDS / STRAINS — steps: ① fix the breeding objective and the target environment ② choose foundation breeds that complement each other ③ crossbreed to the desired level of inheritance ④ INTER-SE MATING of the crossbreds ⑤ rigorous selection and culling for the objective over several generations ⑥ close the flock/herd and stabilise type ⑦ evaluate, expand, register (NBAGR) and disseminate.

19 CONSERVATION OF ANIMAL GENETIC RESOURCES (AnGR) ★★

Conservation = all human activities, including strategies and plans, that ensure the continued existence, use and availability of animal genetic resources. Preservation is the narrower act of holding the material unchanged (usually frozen).

WHY conserve? insurance against unknown future needs (climate, disease, market) · unique adaptation, heat & tick tolerance, disease resistance and draught ability of indigenous breeds · they are the raw material for future selection and crossbreeding · scientific, cultural and socio-economic value to rural livelihoods · ethical duty; a breed lost is lost forever.

CAUSES of genetic erosion: indiscriminate crossbreeding and unregulated grading up · concentration on a few high-yielding exotic breeds · mechanisation replacing draught cattle · loss of common grazing land and changed farming systems · small population size → drift and inbreeding · disease outbreaks, drought and disasters · lack of economic incentive to the keeper.

MethodWhat it means
IN-SITU
(in the native habitat)		Maintaining live populations in their own breeding tract under the farmer's management: breed societies & breeders' associations, herd/flock registration, subsidy and incentive schemes, elite bull production, gaushalas, Gokul Grams, protected breeding tracts, herd books, community/village-level conservation, providing an economic market for the breed's product. Advantage: the breed keeps evolving with its environment and stays useful.
EX-SITU IN-VIVOLive animals maintained outside the breeding tract — government farms, ICAR institute herds, university farms, zoos and rescue centres.
EX-SITU IN-VITRO
(cryoconservation)		Cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen (−196 °C) of semen, embryos, oocytes, somatic cells and DNA in a gene/germplasm bank. Cheap, safe, indefinite; but the breed stops evolving and cannot be used in situ.

Supporting tools: characterisation and registration by NBAGR; FAO DAD-IS global database; breed risk-status classes (extinct, critical, endangered, vulnerable, at risk, not at risk) judged on number of breeding females, Ne and population trend; molecular microsatellite/SNP characterisation to prioritise genetically distinct breeds; Minimum Viable Population and Ne ≥ 50 targets.

21 BREEDING FOR DISEASE RESISTANCE

Resistance = ability of the animal to limit establishment/multiplication of the pathogen. Tolerance = ability to remain productive despite infection. Resilience = ability to recover after challenge.

APPROACHES: ① Direct selection on the disease trait (incidence, clinical score) ② Indirect selection on a correlated indicator — e.g. somatic cell count (SCC) for mastitis (h² 0.10–0.15 vs mastitis incidence 0.02–0.05), FAMACHA/FEC for worm resistance ③ Major genes and MAS ④ crossbreeding to introduce a resistant breed's genes ⑤ transgenesis / gene editing.

24 WILD ANIMALS — POPULATION DYNAMICS, Ne, PLANNED & CONTROLLED BREEDING ★

POPULATION DYNAMICS = study of how a population's size and structure change with time under natality, mortality, immigration and emigration (Nt+1 = Nt + B − D + I − E). Growth is exponential (J-shaped) when resources are unlimited and logistic (S-shaped) when limited by the carrying capacity K. Also studied: age structure & life tables, sex ratio, density-dependent (competition, disease) vs density-independent (drought, fire) factors, Minimum Viable Population and Population Viability Analysis (PVA).

EFFECTIVE POPULATION SIZE Ne = 4 Nm Nf / (Nm + Nf)  ;  ΔF = 1 / (2Ne)

Ne = the size of an idealised population that would show the same rate of inbreeding / loss of genetic variation as the actual population. Ne is almost always much SMALLER than the census number N, and is reduced by: unequal sex ratio (a few breeding males), variance in family size, fluctuating population size (use the harmonic mean — a single bottleneck dominates), overlapping generations and non-random mating. 50/500 rule: Ne ≥ 50 to avoid immediate inbreeding depression (ΔF ≤ 1%/generation) and Ne ≥ 500 to retain long-term evolutionary potential.

PLANNED BREEDING IN CAPTIVITY: maintained through STUDBOOKS (international/national, kept by a studbook keeper) and pedigree analysis. Objectives: retain ~90% of the founders' gene diversity for 100 years, minimise inbreeding, equalise founder representation, avoid adaptation to captivity. Pairings are chosen by MEAN KINSHIP (mate the lowest-MK animals; avoid pairs with high kinship), supported by genome resource banks and cooperative programmes (SSP in the USA, EEP in Europe; in India the Central Zoo Authority conservation-breeding programmes for the Red Panda, Snow Leopard, Sangai (brow-antlered deer), Pygmy Hog, Great Indian Bustard, Gharial and vultures (Jatayu centres)). Longer generation intervals are deliberately encouraged in captive programmes to slow genetic drift — the opposite of livestock practice.

CONTROLLED BREEDING & ASSISTED REPRODUCTION: oestrus detection by non-invasive faecal/urinary steroid monitoring and ultrasonography; oestrus synchronisation and GnRH/hormonal induction; semen collection by electroejaculation, evaluation and cryopreservation in genome resource banks; AI (successful in elephant, rhino, giant panda, big cats), embryo transfer and IVF, interspecies surrogacy, and cloning for critically endangered species; contraception (GnRH vaccines, implants) to prevent surplus animals in over-represented species. Breeding for conservation also requires avoiding hybridisation with related species and outbreeding depression, and planning eventual reintroduction/rewilding with disease screening and behavioural preparation.
